## Short project description

Curbwise helps residents turn everyday street concerns into understandable design concepts and shared priorities. We are developing accessible software and a facilitated community process so people can document barriers, compare possible improvements, and prepare clear materials for local decision-makers.

## Problem

People who use a street every day often know its problems intimately. Turning that experience into a useful planning conversation can require time, specialist vocabulary, and visual tools they do not have. A vague complaint may fail to explain the location, tradeoffs, or specific next step. Curbwise's proposed public-benefit program would help residents participate with clear observations and understandable concepts, including people who cannot attend a conventional meeting or use a map interface independently.

## Product and delivery model

The working prototype includes street cross-section editing, before/after concept views, selected dimensional guidance checks, and PDF generation. Its reporting and community functions need additional work before public deployment. We will begin with a locally hosted pilot, pairing the usable concept tools with supported participation and a documented feedback loop.

Residents and local hosts will select concerns to explore. A facilitator will help participants compare concepts, record disagreements and assumptions, and create an accessible brief. We will track delivery to an agreed recipient, any response, and what participants want to do next. Paper and assisted input will remain available. The tools support discussion; they do not certify engineering designs.

## Pilot and evidence

The first pilot location is flexible. We will choose a community with a committed host, resident leadership, and a realistic funding/delivery arrangement. Proposed 90-day targets are 30–50 distinct participants, 20 location records, five reviewed priorities, two workshops, and one delivered brief. These are goals, not demonstrated traction.

We will measure successful first contributions, repeat participation, time spent producing a usable brief, participant understanding, and recipient feedback. We will test the hypothesis that combining visual tools with facilitation and report-back produces more useful participation than a stand-alone reporting form. We do not yet have evidence of crash reduction, city adoption, or recurring revenue.
